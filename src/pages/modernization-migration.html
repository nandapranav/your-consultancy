10 / THE MIGRATION LAYER

From plan to safe cutover.

A migration plan is a spreadsheet until wave sequencing, dependency mapping, and rollback safety turn it into something you can actually execute.

The riskiest part of modernization is rarely the target architecture—it's the transition. Dependencies between systems, data consistency during cutover, and the blast radius of a failed wave are what turn a straightforward migration into a multi-quarter fire drill.

We help engineer the transition itself: dependency mapping, wave sequencing, cutover runbooks, validation checks, and rollback plans so each move is reversible until it's proven safe to commit to.

01 / DEPENDENCY MAPPING

Understand what talks to what before deciding what moves first, to avoid breaking something upstream.

02 / WAVE SEQUENCING

Group systems into waves that minimize risk and let early waves prove the pattern for later ones.

03 / CUTOVER SAFETY

Runbooks, validation checks, and rollback plans so each wave can be reversed before it's fully committed.

04 / DECOMMISSION

A deliberate plan to retire legacy systems—not just leave them running “just in case” forever.

11 / HOW WE WORK

Risk first. Pattern second.

The right migration pattern depends on what a system actually needs to survive the move, not on a default preference for rehost or refactor.

We assess each system on its own terms: how coupled it is, how critical it is, how much technical debt it carries, and how much business value would justify a deeper rebuild versus a straightforward lift.

From there we sequence the portfolio so low-risk, high-learning moves happen first, proving the migration pattern before it's applied to the systems that can least afford a mistake.

A / PRINCIPLE

Assess before you sequence

Coupling, criticality, and technical debt—not convenience—determine the right pattern for each system.

B / PRINCIPLE

Prove it small, then scale it

Early waves validate the approach on lower-risk systems before it's trusted with the critical ones.

C / PRINCIPLE

Plan the exit, not just the entry

Decommissioning legacy systems is part of the plan from day one, not an afterthought.

12 / REFERENCE SYSTEM

The layers of a modernization program.

Assessment, planning, migration, and optimization are separate layers, each with its own risks and its own exit criteria.

We separate these layers so a delay or surprise in one wave doesn't cascade into the whole program losing momentum or credibility.

01

Assessment & Discovery

Inventory systems, dependencies, technical debt, and business criticality to build an evidence-based baseline.

02

Wave Planning

Sequence systems into waves balancing risk, dependency, and business value.

03

Migration Patterns

Rehost, replatform, refactor, or retire—chosen per system based on its assessed profile.

04

Cutover & Validation

Execute each wave with runbooks, validation checks, and a clear rollback path.

05

Decommission & Optimization

Retire legacy systems deliberately and tune the new environment for cost and performance.

14 / COMMON QUESTIONS

Built around real work.

Is this strategy or engineering?

Both, when both are needed. We use strategy to determine what should happen and engineering depth to make sure the recommendation can survive contact with the real environment.

Do you work with existing teams?

Yes. The strongest engagements pair Fifth Cursor expertise with the people who own the systems and outcomes. We aim to transfer context and capability rather than create permanent dependency.

How do you choose technology?

We start with workload requirements, constraints, risk, economics, team capability, and existing investments. Tools are selected after the problem is understood.

How do projects stay measurable?

We define success before implementation and track operational, adoption, quality, financial, and business indicators throughout delivery.

What happens after the roadmap?

Where useful, we continue into implementation, architecture, platform work, pilots, or delivery support. The goal is momentum—not a binder that gets forgotten.
